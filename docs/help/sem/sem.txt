$#>Secondary Electron Detector


The Secondary Electron Detector (2) is mounted on the rear wall of the chamber.  The electrode  (6) [shown as a grid in the diagram] is provided in front, which is held on positive potential for secondary electron detection and forms a collecting field for secondary electrons around the specimen. They move towards the detector (6).   The scintillator (5) is mounted on light guide (4) inside the chamber.  The scintillator (5)  - is biased to + 8 kV and accelerates the low-energy secondary electrons to a considerably higher energy level. These impinge on the scintillator (5) where they generate photons which the light guide (4) directs out of the chamber to photomultiplier (3). 	{bmc bm29.wmf}	

Photomultiplier (3) amplifies and converts the light current into electron current which represents the secondary video signal on the output of the preamplifier. If the collector is biased to negative potential only backscattered electrons will fall on the scintillator. A backscattered electron image of the specimen surface is produced in this operating mode.

This detector is especially suitable for long working distances. The lateral "illumination" emphasizes the topography.

$#>Depth of Penetration

The higher the high voltage the deeper the penetration, or, the lower the atomic number of the element at the specimen spot the deeper the penetration.


a)	If the electron beam penetrates deeply into the specimen, more information stems from deeper specimen layers than from the surface.

b)	The beam is expanded by scattering when penetrating deeply into the specimen, which impairs the spatial resolution.


First conclusion: High accelerating voltages are useless for chemically "light" material (organic specimens) unless the specimen surface is "sealed" by a metal (gold) layer which will supply the information. 

Second conclusion: Sensitive specimens destroyed by the energy absorbed from the beam require a lower potential to reduce the energy. 
	Exception: 
	Thin foils and membranes where high voltage will transmit the foil so that it absorbs less energy than at low voltage when it absorbs the whole beam.

Third conclusion: For observation of coated insulators the beam should not penetrate the coating (low voltage) to prevent charging of the insulator which is not discharged and could make imaging impossible.

$#>Dynamic Focus Principle

The high depth of focus of the scanning electron microscope is due to the projection of the scanning electron beam on the specimen under an aperture angle of only a few milliradians. the depth-of-focus range can be exceeded if a flat specimen is highly tilted. The edges of the image are then defocused. This becomes obvious especially in imaging with high pixel density when a great number of pixels are scanned.
If the focus plane of the specimen surface follows the scanning beam, defocusing at the edges of the image is eliminated. Dynamic Focus does this automatically if the parameters are correctly adjusted, and the image will be in focus even with high tilt angles.

The position of the electron-beam focus plane relative to the specimen is defined by the current which flows through objective lens in the electron-optical column. The input variable of Dynamic Focus is the saw tooth of image deflection circuit. The power amplifier then supplies a saw tooth current to the objective lens for corresponding modulation of the focal length (dF).   If the deflection starts on top of the image, the lens current is reduced and the focus plane adjusted to a longer working distance (y1); the current is higher at the lower edge of the image and the working distance correspondingly shorter (y2).	      {bmc bm51.wmf}	
{bmc bm12.bmp}	The specimen must be tilted so that the tilt axis is horizontal in the image field; the adjustment can be made 
	with Scan RotationScan_Rotation.

$#>Floppy Disk Drive

Data are stored on disks on concentric tracks. The write/read head of the disk drive moves from track to track while the disk rotates, thereby searching data for reading or blank areas for writing.

The tracks are divided into sectors and bytes. The storage capacity of a disk is given by bytes. One byte is required to store one character. The number of tracks, sectors and bytes of a disk depend on the disk type.

One KByte corresponds to 1024 bytes, one MByte to 1024 KByte.

Loading and removing disks:

{bmc bm20.bmp}	Caution: 	Do not remove disks from the drive as long as drive signal lamp (1) is on, to prevent disk or write/read head from being damaged.

Loading  {bmc bm3.bmp} Load disk in drive {bmc bm3.bmp} Carefully slide in disk as far as it will go  Removing  {bmc bm3.bmp} Operate disk eject key (2) to the lower right of the disk drive. 	 {bmc bm60.wmf}	

See also 	Floppy Disk TypesFloppy_Disk_Types and Formatting Floppy DisksFormatting_Floppy_Disks.
$#>Floppy Disk Types

The 1.44 MB disk drive also accepts 3.5" disks formatted for 720 KB. 

If 3.5" disks are loaded in 3.5" drives of different systems (with different storage capacities), only 1.44 MB disks should be used, which are formatted for 720 KB.  Disks with high storage capacity are designated HD (high density) (1) and characterized by two square recesses (2).   3.5" disks with HD capacity, formatted for 1.44 MB, can only be used in 1.44 MB disk drives.   Disks with lower storage capacity are designated DD (double density) and have only one recess. They may be formatted and used in an HD disk drive. 	   {bmc bm61.wmf}	

New disks must be formatted before use, i.e. prepared for the user's system. Formatting clears all data stored on the disk; the disk is ready to be used for other purposes. 

Write protection  A write-protected disk can be read but not overwritten. This prevents formatting by mistake and overwriting of data.  3.5" disks are provided with a write protect slot in the upper corner. If the slot is open, writing is impossible. Closing the slot by shifting the plastic tongue overrides write protection. 	     {bmc bm62.wmf}

$#>Magnification Principle

Deflection coils in the objective lens move the electron beam in a scan field over the specimen.
The digital control provides for point-by-point scanning of the specimen. 
The size of the scan field on the specimen is variable by adjusting the magnification. The magnification is defined by the ratio of the scan field size on the screen to the scan field size on the specimen.

 If the scan field on the specimen is of the same size as the scan field on the screen, the magnification is 1x as shown in the diagram on the right.     But if the scan field is only half this size, the specimen features on the monitor will be double the size of the previous adjustment.  The smaller the scan field on the specimen the higher the magnification. 	                {bmc bm124.wmf}

$#>Noise Reduction Principle

The signal entering the image processor is made up of two components, image and noise. Image is the signal of interest and correlates with the object being scanned, noise is random in nature.

Therefore by averaging multiple scans of the same area, the signal will be reinforced, while the noise will be reduced. This is the basis on which the noise reduction works.

$#>Resolution

The higher the voltage the smaller the effective beam diameter of the specimen, because

a) the highly energized electromagnetic lenses have less aberrations,

b) the smaller relative chromatic width of the beam reduces the chromatic aberration,

c) the brightness of the source increases which means more electrons in the beam cross section,

d) the beam is deflected less from nominal position by magnetic stray fields.

It generally applies that the smaller the beam diameter the better the lateral resolution, unless this is precluded by any of the reasons mentioned in Section Depth of PenetrationDepth_of_Penetration

$#>Scan Rotation Principle

Scan Rotation rotates the scan field on the specimen, that means the specimen "illumination" rotates along with the specimen. The Scan Rotation axis always coincides with the electron-optical axis so that the specimen tilt does not influence the rotation.
Electrical Scan Rotation is a convenient means to align the edges of the scan field on the specimen. It may be used for better display, but is more important as alignment tool for line scan and measuring system, permitting scanning in adjustable direction without mechanical rotation, especially because mechanical alignment is the more critical the higher the magnification.

Circuit principle Scan Rotation rotates the scan field on the specimen electronically, so that the specimen image rotates in the opposite direction on the monitor. The circuit transforms a right-angled coordinate system x / y into a right-angled coordinate system u / v turned about the angle f, according to the following equations:    u = x cos f - y sin f   v = x sin f - y cos f Both coordinate systems have the same origin in the center of the scan field. The trigonometric functions are generated digitally and graded in degrees. The deflection amplifiers for the electron beam are driven by the deflection voltages u / v after transformation of x / y into u / v.   	      {bmc bm160.wmf}	

$#>Scanning Principle

A narrowly bundled electron beam is moved in a point-by-point scan over the specimen and releases different signals at each pixel of the specimen surface, which are received and evaluated by suitable detectors. The total of scanned pixels produce on the monitor an image of the scanned field.  (1) specimen surface  (2) Frame Store  (3) monitor     	      {bmc bm161.wmf}

$#>Tilt Correction Principle

In the scanning electron microscope all specimen features are projection displays, both on screen and photograph. Tilted surfaces are foreshortened in the tilt direction, that means they are in a way distorted compared with vertical observation direction. The distortion is corrected by shortening the scan field at right angles to the tilt axis and so stretching the image in this direction on screen and photograph, which provides for correction with proper adjustment. This can be achieved only with perfectly flat samples such as polished sections.

If the electron beam moves over the specimen from y1 to y2 it covers the distance L. Tilting the specimen from horizontal position about the angle q displays on the monitor the projection of the distance L, that is L’. The specimen is foreshortened by the factor   L’/L = k = cos q  Foreshortening by this factor the scan field on the specimen reestablishes on the monitor the original distance L. The field is foreshortened by adjustable attenuation of the Y amplitude of the deflection current.  	           {bmc bm212.wmf}	
{bmc bm12.bmp}	Tilt Correction is effective in the Y direction, that means the image is rectified vertically on the screen. The tilt direction must run along the Y axis and the tilt axis of the specimen surface along the X axis.

$#>Use of Different Scan Modes

Scan time and scan mode selection can greatly influence screening procedure and the result of the action.

Fast scans are used if the magnification and the specimen sensitivity permit the generation of a sufficiently strong and noiseless signal. It is used for quick adjustment and screening of specimens, because fast scan images follow immediately the mechanical movement of the specimen. This mode must also be used for dynamic processes, e.g. aperture alignment with focus wobble

 Fast scans can help to produce an image of the surface for the examination of charging specimens, because the generated charge pattern is more homogeneous than in slow acquisition mode.  A signal which is so low that it causes interfering noise will require longer scan times. The noise is then reduced by filtering. The longer the image acquisition time the higher the noise reduction. $#>Vacuum System


The operation of the electron optical column and specimen scanning is dependent not only on the presence of a vacuum, but also the degree of vacuum, i.e. the system is safely interlocked so that it is not possible to switch on any of the high voltages until an adequate vacuum is reached.

The vacuum system is simple and efficient. A turbomolecular pump, backed by a rotary pump, is mounted on the lower face of the specimen chamber and commences pumping when the system is being evacuated and comes to rest during system venting. The electron gun and column are pumped through the tube running from the chamber up to the gun section. The only valve in this system is the air-admittance valve attached to the rear of the chamber, which automatically opens when venting is requested. Normal operating vacuum should be in the range to   10-4 to 10-6 torr.  No water cooling is required.
See SEM Control VacuumC9V9DR@tabmgr.hlp


$#>Column and Chamber


The LEO 420/430 gun and column19A.J.D@maint.hlp is used to generate and focus a fine beam of electrons onto the surface of a specimen mounted in the specimen chamber. The electron gun has a thermionic emitter as a source of electrons - the source is a tungsten hairpin filament. Three electromagnetic lenses beneath the gun, focus and shape the electron beam before it strikes the specimen in a scanned, or rastered, fashion. The energy of the electron beam is adjustable from 300 volts to 30 kilovolts in 10 volt steps and the electron probe current is continuously adjustable from 1 pico-amp to 1 micro-amp to suit the type of examination required.

The specimen chamber of the LEO 430 holds the specimen to be viewed in such a way that it may be freely manoeuvred during examination. The specimen stage which holds the specimen is attached to the door of the chamber and is of the manual cartesian type on the LEO 420 and is a 5 axis motorised cartesian type on the LEO 430.  The stage may be opened for examination once the vacuum within the chamber has been vented.
